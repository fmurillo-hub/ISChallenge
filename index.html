The SME Technology Challenge

Group consulting assignment and presentation. Can your team turn a business problem into a realistic information system proposal?

Weight15% of the final course grade
Groups3–4 students. Every member contributes to the analysis and presents.
DeliverablesAn eight-slide presentation with an appendix for detailed calculations.
Presentation6–8 minutes, followed by 3 minutes of questions.
Class session80 minutes
What you are doingProposing a solution. You are not required to build or purchase software.
1 · The assignment

The Assignment

Your task

You are a team of consultants advising a small or medium-sized enterprise (SME). Your assigned company is struggling with an important area of its business. Management believes technology could help, but it needs a clear explanation of what to change, why it matters, and whether the investment is worthwhile.

Your group must identify and justify the main business need or opportunity, propose an information system, and explain its technical, financial, and organizational implications.

Fictional figures

All companies and figures in this document are fictional and created for this activity. All budgets are in US dollars. Budget limits represent what each company can afford, not actual software prices.

Final challenge

Convince the owner that your proposal addresses the right problem, can work within the company’s constraints, and offers benefits worth the cost.

Learning outcomes

By completing this assignment you will be able to:

  • Distinguish a visible symptom from its underlying cause, using evidence.
  • Identify the information a business needs and trace how it should move between roles.
  • Design an information system using the four components (people, processes, data, technology) and the IS cycle.
  • Classify a system as operational, tactical or strategic, and explain its competitive value using Porter’s Five Forces.
  • Defend technology choices: integrated or specialized, immediate or verified, automatic or approved.
  • Build a realistic budget, separate cash savings from time released and from contribution, and set measurable KPIs.
  • Communicate a recommendation to a decision-maker in a short, well-structured presentation.

Ground rules

1

There is no single correct solution.

Your recommendation must fit the company’s size, resources, employees and priorities.

2

“The company needs better technology” is never a sufficient answer.

Name the problem, the information, the process and the system.

3

A brand name alone is not a solution.

Describe the functions the business needs and how employees would use them. If you cite real vendor prices, give the source, date, plan and number of users.

4

Do not assume systems connect automatically.

Any integration you rely on must be flagged as something to verify.

5

Label assumptions.

Say what you assumed and what you would confirm with management.

6

AI is optional.

If you include it, state its precise role, the data it needs, possible errors and the human oversight.

5 · Concept primer

Concept Primer

These are the concepts the assignment expects you to use correctly. Use them as tools to explain your case, not as vocabulary to list.

Information technology (IT) and information system (IS)

IT is the hardware, software and networks. An IS is the combination of people, processes, data and technology that produces useful information for decisions and actions. A barcode scanner is IT. The routine in which scans update stock, trigger an order proposal and are checked by a manager is part of an IS.

The four components of an IS
ComponentWhat you must explain
PeopleUsers, responsibilities, training, ownership of the system
ProcessesWorkflows, approvals and rules that must change
DataRecords required, quality checks, storage and access
TechnologySoftware, devices, connectivity and connections to existing tools

A proposal that covers only the technology is incomplete. Most failures come from people, processes or data.

The IS cycle: Input, Processing, Output, Feedback

Pick one business event and walk it through the whole cycle.

InputData enters (a sale is scanned).
ProcessingThe system applies rules (stock is reduced; stock falls below the reorder level).
OutputInformation is produced (a proposed order and a low-stock alert).
FeedbackResults are compared with expectations and lead to a correction (the manager sees that the order was too large and adjusts the reorder level).

Feedback is the step that is most often left vague. Make it concrete: who sees what result, and what do they change?

System categories
CategoryTypical purposeTypical question it answers
Transaction processing system (TPS)Record everyday events accurately (sales, payments, bookings)What happened?
CRMManage customer contact, follow-up, history and complaintsWho is this customer and what do they need next?
Inventory systemTrack stock levels and movementsWhat do we have, where, and how much?
ERPIntegrate core functions (sales, purchasing, production, finance) on shared dataAre all departments working from the same record?
BI / reportingSummarise and visualise data for managersHow are we doing and what should we decide?
Collaboration systemShare files, tasks and decisions across a teamWho owns what, and which version is final?
Operational, tactical and strategic systems

Operational systems support daily transactions and routine work. Tactical systems support middle managers who monitor performance and allocate resources over weeks or months. Strategic systems support senior decisions about direction, positioning and long-term advantage. Classify your proposal as primarily one level and state whether it also supports another. A system that captures daily data (operational) and feeds a weekly review (tactical) supports two levels.

Porter’s Five Forces

Choose one force that is most relevant to your case and explain how the system helps the company respond to it. Naming the force is not enough.

ForceQuestionHow an IS might respond (example)
Competitive rivalryHow intensely do direct competitors fight for the same customers?Faster response, better service, better availability
Threat of new entrantsHow easily can a newcomer enter?Customer data and relationships that are hard to copy
Threat of substitutesCan customers meet the same need another way?Convenience and service that substitutes cannot match
Bargaining power of buyersCan customers demand more or walk away easily?Visibility, reliability, switching costs through service quality
Bargaining power of suppliersDo suppliers control price, terms or timing?Better demand data to plan and negotiate orders

Also ask: could a competitor copy the technology easily? If so, what else (data, process discipline, relationships, service culture) would sustain your advantage?

The three debates
DebateThink about
One integrated system or specialized systems?Integrated: shared data, fewer mismatches, but higher cost, longer implementation and more change at once. Specialized: faster, cheaper and easier to learn, but information must be connected, and each connection is a risk to verify.
Immediate information or verified information?Speed matters where a decision cannot wait (a driver’s delivery update, available stock for an order). Accuracy matters where an error is costly or hard to reverse (financial reports, invoices, approvals).
Automatic decisions or human approval?Automate rules that are clear, frequent and low risk (reminders, alerts, routine reorders). Keep humans for judgment, exceptions, unusual amounts and relationships.
Benefits: do not mix cash, time and sales
Type of benefitHow to treat itExample of the correct reasoning
Actual cash savingsMoney the company will stop spending or loseFewer emergency purchases at a higher price: count the price difference you expect to avoid
Employee time releasedHours that can be used for other work. Not cash unless hours are actually cut or overtime avoided.Reporting takes 20 hours less per month: say what that time will be used for, and value it separately or not at all
Additional contribution from new salesExtra units or bookings times the contribution margin, not the revenueTen more jobs at a $30 contribution is $300, not the full selling price
Mini-example

A bakery loses sales when it runs out of popular bread by noon. Symptom: unhappy customers. Underlying cause (assumption to confirm): bakers decide quantities from memory, with no record of daily sales by product. Information need: daily sales by product and hour, from the till, needed by the head baker each evening to set tomorrow’s batch. IS cycle: input (till records each sale), processing (summary of the last four weeks by weekday), output (suggested batch sizes), feedback (leftover and sell-out times are compared with the suggestion and the baker adjusts the rule). Level: operational with a tactical weekly review. Benefit: if 15 extra loaves sell per week at a $0.40 contribution each, that is $6 a week of contribution, not revenue and not cash saved. This is the style of reasoning expected for your own case.

7 · Required group analysis

Required Group Analysis

Use the following structure for your proposal. Each part tells you what to include and what a strong answer looks like. The parts map to the eight slides in Section 8.

1

Identify and justify the need or opportunity

Write a clear problem statement explaining:

  • What is going wrong or what opportunity exists.
  • Who is affected.
  • Why it matters to the business.
  • What the company should prioritize.

Use at least three pieces of evidence from your case. Distinguish between a visible symptom and a likely underlying cause. Label your assumptions and list the information you would need to confirm with management.

Weak“The company needs better technology.”
Strong“Brilla loses sales because no one owns follow-up: 70 of 240 inquiries waited over 24 hours, and only 12 of 40 recurring customers received reminders. The underlying cause is likely that customer information lives in separate phones (assumption to confirm).”
2

Identify the information needs

Identify at least five important data items your proposed system requires. For each one, complete the table (template in Appendix A):

  • Data needed
  • Source or person entering it
  • Who needs it
  • When it is needed
  • Decision or action it supports

Explain how you would address missing, duplicated, inaccurate or outdated data. Think of a rule, a check or a responsible person, not just “clean the data”.

3

Map the current and proposed process

Create two simple process maps: the current process (how work and information move today) and the proposed process (after your changes). Each should have approximately five to seven steps. On the maps, identify:

  • The main bottleneck.
  • The people involved.
  • At least one handoff between roles or departments.

Then explain which working procedures must change for your proposal to succeed. Draw the maps as simple boxes and arrows on a slide. Each box should be a verb phrase (“Store manager counts stock”), with the person responsible named.

4

Propose and explain an information system

Identify the system category or combination of categories you recommend (CRM, ERP, BI, inventory, transaction processing, collaboration or another). Explain the functions the business needs and how employees would use them. Cover all four components (People, Processes, Data, Technology; see Section 5).

Then demonstrate one business event through the full IS cycle: Input, Processing, Output, Feedback. Make the feedback step concrete: explain how information about results leads to a correction or improvement.

Finally, classify the proposal as primarily operational, tactical or strategic, and say whether it also supports another level.

5

Explain the competitive value

Show how the proposal could improve efficiency, customer experience, differentiation or managerial decisions. Apply one relevant force from Porter’s Five Forces and explain how the system helps the company respond to it. Consider whether competitors could easily copy the technology and what else the company would need to sustain an advantage.

6

Defend your technology choices

Address each class debate (see the table in Section 5):

  • One integrated system or specialized systems: explain the benefits, limitations, and the information connections required.
  • Immediate information or verified information: identify where speed matters most and where checking accuracy should come first.
  • Automatic decisions or human approval: give one example of an automated action and one decision requiring human judgment.

If a debate has limited relevance to your case, explain why. If you include AI, identify its precise role, required data, potential errors and human oversight.

7

Evaluate technical and organizational implications

Explain each of the following:

  • Required devices and internet connectivity.
  • Whether the system would run through a cloud service or local installation.
  • How existing records would be cleaned and transferred.
  • How the system would exchange information with current tools.
  • User permissions, backups, and protection of sensitive data.
  • What employees would do during an outage.
  • Who would provide support and maintenance.
  • How staff would learn and adopt the new process.

Do not assume systems connect automatically. Identify any integration capability that would need verification.

8

Build a realistic cost estimate

Prepare a budget covering both implementation and the first year of operation (template in Appendix A).

9

Propose implementation and measure success

Develop a simple 90-day implementation plan covering preparation, a small pilot, training and rollout. Include:

  • A responsible person for each phase.
  • A condition the pilot must meet before expansion.
  • Three major risks and a response to each.
  • Three KPIs with a baseline, proposed target and measurement method.
KPICurrent baselineProposed 90-day targetHow it will be measured

If the case does not provide a baseline, explain how you would establish it. Finish with a clear recommendation: proceed, pilot first, or postpone until a specific condition is met.

The 7:10 total leaves about 50 seconds of buffer for handoffs between speakers. If you are consistently under 6:00, you are probably omitting reasoning. If you are over 7:30 in rehearsal, cut words, not numbers.

Preparing for the three minutes of questions

Expect questions like these. Prepare short, evidence-based answers and decide who will take which:

  • Why did you prioritize this problem over the others in the case?
  • What would you do first if you could only spend half the budget?
  • What happens if the integration you are relying on does not work?
  • Which of your benefits is actual cash and which is not?
  • What is the biggest risk to adoption, and who will own it?
  • Could a competitor copy this next month? What would still protect the company?
  • Why a human approval here and an automatic action there?
  • What would make you change your recommendation from proceed to postpone?

If you do not know, say so, explain what you would check and who you would ask. Honest, specific uncertainty scores better than a confident guess.
